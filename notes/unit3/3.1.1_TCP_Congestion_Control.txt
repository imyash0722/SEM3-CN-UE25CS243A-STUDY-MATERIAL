History

In October 1986 the link between LBL (Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory) and
UC Berkeley — 400 metres apart — dropped from 32 kbps to 40 bps. A
thousandfold collapse, and the network hardware was fine. Every sender
was retransmitting into a queue that was already full, so the network spent
all its capacity carrying copies of packets that had already been delivered.
Van Jacobson's fix is what you are learning today
It's 9pm in the hostel. Netflix is buffering. Is that a flow control problem or a
congestion control problem — and how would you tell the difference from
where you're sitting?

ECN: signalling congestion without dropping packets

1

Router is congested. Instead of dropping the packet, it sets the CE bit (Congestion Experienced) — two bits in the IP
header's ToS field. The packet is still delivered.

2

Receiver sees CE. It sets the ECE flag (ECN-Echo) in the TCP header of its very next ACK.

3

Sender sees ECE. It halves cwnd exactly as if it had detected loss, and sets CWR (Congestion Window Reduced) to confirm it
acted.

4

Router, sender and receiver must all support ECN — which is why a mechanism specified in 1999 took two decades to see
real deployment.

Congestion signalled, zero packets destroyed. But the feedback still costs a full round trip, and the network itself had to be
modified to send it. That deployment cost is exactly why TCP chose end-to-end inference instead.

TCP: Determine sender rate
Too fast – congestion collapse
Too cautious and too slowly – under utilize the bandwidth
Send at a high rate without congesting the network
Guiding principles:
• A lost segment implies congestion, and hence, the TCP sender’s rate should be decreased
when a segment is lost.
• An acknowledged segment indicates that the network is delivering the sender’s segments to
the receiver, and hence, the sender’s rate can be increased when an ACK arrives for a
previously unacknowledged segment.
• Bandwidth probing.

TCP congestion-control algorithm
(1) slow start, (2) congestion avoidance, and (3) fast recovery

TCP: from slow start to congestion avoidance
Initial ssthresh = 8 MSS; loss at end of round 8 via triple duplicate ACK, so ssthresh becomes 6:
Rounds 1–4 are slow start (doubling to ssthresh = 8). Rounds 5–8 are congestion avoidance (+1/RTT).
At round 8 both compute ssthresh = 6;
Tahoe drops to 1 and re-climbs through slow start, Reno resumes at 6 in congestion avoidance. That
gap is the throughput Tahoe gives away.

TCP CUBIC: a better way to probe for bandwidth
+1 MSS per RTT is glacial once cwnd is thousands of segments. To hold 10 Gbps over a 100 ms path,
Reno needs a loss rate near 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ — one loss in five billion segments.
Insight: Wₘₐₓ is the window at which loss was last detected — the same value Reno uses to set ssthresh. The bottleneck has
probably not changed much since then, so return to Wₘₐₓ quickly, then approach it with care.

Reno's problem

Return fast
Climb steeply back toward the last known limit
instead of crawling up one segment per RTT.

Then hold
Flatten out just below Wₘₐₓ instead of sailing
through into the next loss.

Back off less
CUBIC cuts to 0.70 × Wₘₐₓ, not to half. A gentler
cut means less to climb back.
More area under the curve = higher
throughput.

TCP CUBIC: the shape of the curve
A cube is steep, then flat, then steep again

Slide the flat point up to Wₘₐₓ — that is CUBIC.

K — the deadline
The time by which CUBIC intends to be back at
Wₘₐₓ. Not a fixed number — the further the
window had to drop, the further away K sits.

The curve depends on elapsed time, not on how many ACKs have come back.

Why elapsed time matters.
Under Reno a 20 ms flow grows ten times faster
than a 200 ms flow, and takes a far larger share.
Under CUBIC both follow this same curve.
